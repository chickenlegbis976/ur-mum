## COLD OPEN — [0:00–0:45]

[B-ROLL: slow push down a narrow stone street in Porto. Overcast. No music yet.]

Porto, Portugal. January second, 1890.

A man is dying in a room at the Grand Hotel de Paris.

His name is José António de Sampaio Júnior. He'd come up from Lisbon at Christmas to see his father. Now he's vomiting blood.

[B-ROLL: period photograph of the hotel or a 19th-century Porto hotel interior]

There's a doctor at the bedside. Not a stranger. Family. José's own brother-in-law.

By most accounts of this case, the doctor suggests the vomit be cleaned away. It is.

The death gets written down as cerebral congestion. Natural causes. No autopsy. No questions.

[B-ROLL: close on a period death register, handwriting]

Three months later a boy in that same family dies too.

And that time, somebody keeps the evidence. Seals it in jars.

**[RETENTION BEAT — OPEN LOOP @ 0:38]**
Those jars sit in a laboratory in Porto for the next hundred and thirty-two years. Until someone opens them.

[TITLE CARD: THE CRIME OF FLORES STREET]

## ACT ONE — SETUP — [0:45–3:00]

[B-ROLL: map of Portugal, push in on Porto. Then Flores Street, modern and archival.]

To understand what happened, you need the family. And you need the money.

The Sampaios were wealthy. The grandparents — José António Sampaio and Maria Carolina — kept a house on Rua das Flores. Flores Street. A good address in the middle of Porto.

[B-ROLL: exterior of the Flores Street house — a photo of it survives in the published research]

Living in that house were three children. Mário and Maria Augusta, whose father Guilherme had died. And Berta, their cousin — the daughter of José Júnior, the man in the hotel room.

So by January 1890, those three kids had lost their fathers.

[B-ROLL: family tree graphic — build it on screen, one name at a time]

Now the other side of the family.

In 1877, the Sampaios' daughter, Maria das Dores, married a doctor named Vicente Urbino de Freitas. He married her the same year he qualified.

And he was not a small man in that city. He became a professor at the Medical-Surgical School of Porto. He built a reputation treating leprosy. Respected. Established. The kind of physician a family calls when someone is sick.

[B-ROLL: his portrait — one survives and is reproduced in the academic literature]

That standing matters, and not for the reason you'd think. It's not that his reputation protected him. It's that it made him useful. He was the doctor this family trusted. He was the one they sent for. When a Sampaio got sick, he was in the room.

[B-ROLL: period medical bag, stethoscope, doctor's ledger]

Here's the part that matters.

The Sampaio fortune had to go somewhere. And the people standing between Urbino de Freitas's wife and that money were a short list.

Her brother, José Júnior. And three children.

[B-ROLL: the family tree again — figures dimming out one by one]

José Júnior was already gone. Cerebral congestion, they said. In a hotel room, with his brother-in-law attending.

Nobody thought anything of it.

Not yet.

## ACT TWO — THE EVENT — [3:00–6:00]

[B-ROLL: calendar / Easter imagery, period Portuguese confectionery]

Saturday, March twenty-ninth, 1890. The eve of Palm Sunday.

A parcel arrives at the house on Flores Street.

It came by mail. From Lisbon. The sender's name on it is Lúcio Martins.

Nobody in that house knows a Lúcio Martins. Investigators never found one either.

[B-ROLL: close on a period parcel, twine, wax, a written address]

It's addressed to Berta. The youngest.

Inside: Easter treats. Almonds — described in the sources as almonds with liquor — and cakes made with coconut and chocolate.

And here's the detail I can't get past. Several accounts give the contents as three boxes of almonds and three cakes.

Three.

[B-ROLL: three small boxes in a row, static shot, hold]

There were three children living in that house.

Think about what that means about whoever packed it. They weren't sending sweets to a household. They were sending one to each child.

[B-ROLL: the sweets — recreate as still-life, do NOT dramatize children eating]

The children eat them.

And the children get sick.

[MUSIC: drop out]

The exact sequence over the next few days is not something I'm going to invent for you. What the record establishes is this: the children were poisoned, and the eldest of them did not survive.

Mário Guilherme Augusto de Sampaio died on the second of April, 1890.

**[FLAG — UNCERTAINTY]** Sources conflict on his age. The detailed Portuguese accounts say fourteen. At least one summary of the academic work says twelve. I can't resolve it, so I'm telling you both.

[B-ROLL: a single date card — 2 APRIL 1890]

There's one more thing in the accusation, and it's the detail that turned suspicion into a case.

Two of the girls and the children's grandmother were also said to have been targets. And the reason they survived, according to the charge, is that they refused to take what the family doctor prescribed them.

They wouldn't take the medicine.

[B-ROLL: period medicine bottles, apothecary shelf]

The family doctor was Vicente Urbino de Freitas.

On the sixteenth of April, 1890 — two weeks after Mário's death — he was arrested.

The case was formally filed a week later, on the twenty-third.

## ACT THREE — THE INVESTIGATION — [6:00–10:00]

[B-ROLL: 19th-century laboratory, glassware, gas burners]

Now. This is where it gets messy, and I want to be honest about how messy.

Two bodies were examined. José Júnior — the man from the hotel — and Mário.

The organs went to the Municipal Chemistry Laboratory in Porto. The man doing the work was António Joaquim Ferreira da Silva, a chemistry professor at the Porto Polytechnic School.

[B-ROLL: portrait of Ferreira da Silva; the Porto laboratory, which still exists and has been restored]

The report came back on the seventh of October, 1890. Six months of work.

José Júnior's organs gave them nothing. No alkaloids at all. The lab put that down to how far the body had decomposed.

Dead end. The first death stayed exactly what it had been written down as.

[B-ROLL: a redacted or blank page]

But Mário's organs — and his urine — came back positive.

Three substances. Morphine. Narceine — another compound from opium. And, they said, possibly delphinine, which comes from a plant.

**[RETENTION BEAT @ 7:30 — restate the loop]** Hold onto those three names. Morphine, narceine, delphinine. They are the entire case. And they are the reason we're still talking about this in 2026.

[B-ROLL: three chemical structure diagrams appearing on screen]

Because here's what that finding did.

It turned a family tragedy into a scientific event. Ferreira da Silva's work on this case pushed Portuguese toxicology forward — the research led him toward new detection reactions for other alkaloids, and he helped refine a reagent for identifying morphine. This case is routinely described as the birth of forensic toxicology in Portugal.

The science was on trial next to the man.

[B-ROLL: period courtroom, newspaper front pages]

And the rest of the evidence was a mess.

The parcel came from Lisbon. So the prosecution needed Urbino de Freitas near Lisbon to post it. The defence needed him in Porto.

Witnesses contradicted each other about where he was on the twenty-eighth of March. That single question — Lisbon or Porto — never got a clean answer.

[B-ROLL: two city name cards, splitting the screen]

And that's the whole problem with the physical evidence. There's no fingerprint on that parcel. No handwriting match anyone could rely on. No Lúcio Martins to find and question. The parcel is the murder weapon, and the parcel is anonymous.

[B-ROLL: the parcel again, this time in shadow]

So the case came down to two things. Where a man was standing on one particular day. And what a chemist said he found in a boy's organs.

That's it. That's the case.

[B-ROLL: newspaper archives, stacked case files]

The process dragged on for three and a half years. The record it generated was enormous — the trial hearings alone were published as a book at the time, and the case drew press attention well beyond Portugal. It split public opinion in the country, and it stayed split.

On the first of December, 1893, at the criminal court of São João Novo in Porto, Vicente Urbino de Freitas was convicted of the murder of his nephew Mário.

Note the wording there. Convicted — of Mário's murder.

He was suspected in the death of José Júnior. He was accused of attempting to kill the girls and the grandmother. But the murder conviction was for the boy.

[B-ROLL: text on screen — CONVICTED: 1 count. Murder of Mário Sampaio.]

And the jury was not unanimous. Not everyone in that room believed it.

The sentence: eight years in prison, plus twenty years of exile. He appealed — to the Court of Appeal and to the Supreme Court.

He lost. And the sentence got longer, not shorter. Nine years.

[B-ROLL: prison exterior; then a ship, then Luanda]

He went into the Lisbon Penitentiary in May 1894. Around the start of 1901, he was deported to Luanda, in Angola.

He was stripped of his position. He was barred from practising medicine. The professor was finished.

His wife, Maria das Dores, stayed with him. By the accounts of the case, she never left his side — which is worth sitting with, because if the prosecution was right, the man she stood by had poisoned her own brother and her own nephew for her inheritance.

[B-ROLL: portrait of Maria das Dores — one survives alongside his]

He spent twenty years insisting he was innocent.

In 1913, he was finally allowed to come home to Portugal. He was still trying to get the case reviewed.

He died about three weeks after he got back. Pneumonia.

[B-ROLL: a grave, or a period obituary column]

And that's where the story sat. For a century.

A convicted poisoner. A dead boy. A famous case that Portuguese law students learned about, and almost nobody outside Portugal had ever heard of.

## ACT FOUR — THE TURN — [10:00–12:30]

[B-ROLL: modern laboratory, mass spectrometry equipment, archival boxes]

**[LOOP CLOSES HERE]**

Start of the 2000s. A Portuguese forensic toxicologist named Ricardo Dinis-Oliveira starts pulling the file apart.

Not for a week. For fourteen years. Collecting documents from archives in multiple countries — the autopsy reports, the toxicology reports, the trial record, the forensic psychiatry material.

[B-ROLL: stacks of archival documents, gloved hands]

And the material survived. That's the remarkable part. Physical samples from 1890 still existed.

In 2022, he and his colleagues ran modern elemental analysis on biological samples from the case — a hundred and thirty-two years after they were taken. Dinis-Oliveira also obtained authorization for what he has described as the first autopsy in Portugal performed on a body that old.

[B-ROLL: the 2023 paper's title page — "elemental analysis of biological samples 132 years later"]

So: does the 1890 chemistry hold up?

No.

[MUSIC: stop]

Speaking to CNN Portugal in January 2022 — and I'm quoting this in translation from the Portuguese — Dinis-Oliveira said that after reviewing all of it, he concluded that narceine and delphinine, at minimum, could not have been found. That the conclusions of the Porto experts were wrong.

His position is that a laboratory error produced a result that convicted a man.

[B-ROLL: the three chemical structures from earlier — two of them dissolving away]

Two of the three substances. Gone.

**[FLAG — WHAT THIS DOES AND DOESN'T MEAN]** Be careful here, because this is where a story like this usually overreaches. This is one researcher's conclusion, published and argued in the scientific literature. It is not a court ruling. No Portuguese court has ever overturned the conviction. Vicente Urbino de Freitas died a convicted man and remains one.

What Dinis-Oliveira has said is narrower, and more unsettling: on the evidence as it actually stood, the suspect today would not be convicted.

[B-ROLL: the empty Flores Street house facade]

Which leaves the thing nobody can fix.

Somebody sent that parcel. Somebody used a name that didn't belong to a real person. A boy ate what was in it and died.

That still happened.

## CLOSE — [12:30–14:00]

[B-ROLL: the restored Porto laboratory, then modern Porto]

Here's what I keep coming back to.

The chemistry in this case may have been wrong. But the case built the field. Portuguese forensic toxicology traces its origins to this investigation. The laboratory work here pushed real advances in alkaloid detection that outlived everyone involved. The published research goes further — it credits this case with prompting the medico-legal system Portugal still uses.

An entire scientific discipline in one country grew, in part, out of an analysis that a modern specialist now says was flawed.

That's not a scandal. That's just how new science works when you point it at a courtroom for the first time. Somebody has to go first, and going first means the method is being invented on the same day it's being used to decide whether a man walks out of the building.

The problem is that the man doesn't get a second trial while the method catches up.

[B-ROLL: Ferreira da Silva's lab, glassware in vitrines]

And the questions that are actually open are still open.

We don't know who posted the parcel. We don't know whether José Júnior was murdered at all — his organs were too decomposed to say, and they were too decomposed in 1890. We don't know whether the man who spent nine years in prison and twenty in exile did it.

What we know is that a jury that couldn't agree convicted him on chemistry that a specialist now disputes, in a trial where nobody could establish which city he was standing in.

[B-ROLL: archival portrait of Urbino de Freitas, held on screen]

In April 2026, the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Porto released a miniseries about the case. It came out on the second of April — a hundred and thirty-six years to the day since Mário died.

They're still working on it.

[B-ROLL: slow pull back from Flores Street, wide]

The jars gave up their answer.

Just not the one anyone was waiting for.

[END CARD / OUTRO]

# ACCURACY NOTES — READ BEFORE RECORDING

**Verify these before publishing.** I was unable to open any source page directly — the research environment blocked outbound page fetches, so every fact above comes from search-engine summaries *of* those sources rather than from the source text itself. The sourcing is consistent across independent sources, but you should open the links and confirm before this goes out.

Specific items to check:

- **Mário's age.** Sources conflict: 14 (Portuguese press) vs 12 (a summary of the academic work). The script says both out loud.
- **The sentence.** Original: 8 years + 20 years' exile. After appeal: 9 years. Confirm which figure the court records actually carry.
- **The vomit detail** in the cold open comes from Portuguese popular/genealogical history, not the peer-reviewed papers. The script hedges it ("by most accounts"). If you can't confirm it in a primary source, cut the line — it's the single most dramatic claim in the open and the weakest-sourced.
- **The Dinis-Oliveira quote** is rendered in translation from a Portuguese interview I could not read in the original. The script explicitly says it's a translation. Get a native reading of the CNN Portugal piece before you put the words in his mouth.
- **The 2026 miniseries premiere date** (2 April 2026) and the "136 years" framing should be double-checked against the FMUP announcement.
- **"Three boxes / three cakes / three children."** Both counts are sourced separately. The link between them — one parcel item per child — is *my inference*, and the script frames it that way out loud ("think about what that means"). It is not a finding from the case file. If you want zero inference in the cut, drop the beat.
- **Maria das Dores standing by him** is drawn from Portuguese accounts describing her "unconditional dedication." The script says "by the accounts of the case." Don't upgrade it to a documented fact.
- **"Published as a book at the time" / "press attention beyond Portugal."** The trial hearings do exist as a period volume (*Audiências de Julgamento do Dr. Urbino de Freitas*, listed by Portuguese antiquarian booksellers) and the academic papers describe national and international repercussion. Both are secondhand to me — confirm.
- **He was not charged over José Júnior.** He was suspected. The conviction was one count, for Mário. If an edit ever makes it sound like two murders were proven, that's a factual error, not a stylistic one.

**Deliberate choices:**
- No invented dialogue, interior thoughts, weather, or sensory detail anywhere in the script.
- "Convicted" is used only for Mário's murder. "Suspected" for José Júnior. "Accused of attempting" for the girls and grandmother. Do not let a re-write blur these.
- Victims are named because they are named throughout peer-reviewed literature and have been dead 136 years. If you'd rather not name the surviving children (Berta, Maria Augusta), the parcel line can become "addressed to the youngest girl in the house" with no loss.
- The close does not claim he was innocent. It claims the conviction rests on chemistry a modern specialist disputes. That distinction is load-bearing and legally correct.
